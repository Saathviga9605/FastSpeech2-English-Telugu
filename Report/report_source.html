{{STATUS_LINE}}

1. Implementation approach

I started from the ming024 FastSpeech2 implementation (commit d4e79eb) and ported it into src/, leaving the upstream clone unmodified. I configured and trained two separate models, each with its own config.yaml and phone vocabulary.

Components used from the reference implementation

  • Encoder with 4 layers and decoder with 6 layers, 2 attention heads, 256 hidden units, convolutional feed-forward blocks with kernels 9 and 1, and dropout 0.2.
  • Variance adaptor with duration, pitch and energy predictors (256 filters, kernel 3, dropout 0.5) and 256 linearly quantized pitch and energy embeddings.
  • F0 pitch and energy averaged per phoneme, Montreal Forced Aligner durations, a Tacotron-2-style Post-Net, and the universal HiFi-GAN vocoder.

Differences from the FastSpeech 2 paper

I inherited these from upstream: the later paper versions use continuous wavelet pitch spectrograms rather than F0, the Post-Net is not part of the paper's model, and pitch and energy are predicted per phoneme rather than per frame. The 6-layer decoder is upstream's setting. The Post-Net can be switched off in the config.

English modifications

  • ARPAbet phones from the LibriSpeech lexicon, with g2p_en for unknown words.
  • Homographs resolved by part of speech at inference, needed for E2 (conduct).
  • Times, dates and numbers expanded to words at inference, with every transformation logged.
  • Forced alignment uses a minimum of three states per phone (section 2.3).

Telugu modifications

  • A new rule-based grapheme-to-phoneme frontend (src/text/telugu.py): inherent vowels, vowel signs, virama, and homorganic nasal for anusvara. I designed these rules and checked them with tests, but not against a reference lexicon.
  • Telugu number, ordinal and time expansion for T4.
  • ASCII word keys for MFA, so its tokenizer cannot alter Telugu text. Training text with characters that have no defined pronunciation is rejected rather than cleaned silently.

2.2 Preprocessing

Audio is converted to mono 22,050 Hz 16-bit PCM and peak-normalized. Mel spectrograms use FFT 1024, hop 256, window 1024, 80 bands and 0 to 8000 Hz. F0 uses WORLD DIO and StoneMask, with unvoiced frames interpolated; F0 and energy are averaged per phone and z-normalized with training statistics. Edge silence is trimmed from each clip using the alignment, and internal silence is kept.

A negative result. The first English alignment passed these contrasts and still had a serious defect: MFA's default topology lets a phone last one 10 ms frame, and TH (99.9%), G (98.1%) and R (85.3%) collapsed to that length. Requiring three states raised their medians to 80, 70 and 50 ms. I do not know the cause of the collapse, and a minimum duration does not prove the boundaries are right.

6. Variance adaptor analysis

The values below come from Results/<language>/synthesis_report.json, and the plots follow the discussion. Pitch and energy are per phone. F0 was interpolated across unvoiced frames during training, so values on unvoiced phones such as K or S are not real voicing and should be read with care. Given the English validation results in section 4, I read the English contours as model behaviour rather than as evidence of correct prosody.

Patterns across all sentences

  • Energy: in all ten sentences the maximum falls on a vowel. Minima fall on stops, affricates and fricatives, with one exception: the final vowel of T1. This is the expected broad pattern and fits energy being the only English predictor that beats the per-phone baseline.
  • Duration: no phone is predicted at zero length. English phone means are 68 to 77 ms and Telugu 58 to 67 ms. In Telugu, word-initial vowels are often the longest phones.
  • Pitch: All Telugu sentences end in a clear fall, reaching about 103 to 117 Hz. English sentences fall less consistently, and some contain large jumps inside a sentence.

Telugu generalizes clearly better on these measures. This does not show that the model handles Telugu better than English as a language, because the datasets differ in the way that matters most: English is an unlabelled mix of voices and Telugu trains under one label. Text cannot predict which speaker a clip came from, which would limit English regardless of the model. The step counts also differ, though validation had flattened well before either stopped, so this is unlikely to explain the gap. I did not test either hypothesis with a controlled experiment.

What I heard. T1 and T4 came out very well. The two English problems I heard, in E3 and E4, trace to how the frontend read the text rather than to the acoustic model. The English long word in E1 and the numbers in E4 were understood despite the poor validation scores, so those scores do not by themselves show that the speech is unintelligible. Overall, some sentences sounded a bit robotic, and in some the last word was cut off. I did not record which clips these were, rate quality clip by clip, or compare the two voices in a controlled way. The waveforms do not end at full level: the last 50 ms of each clip is at 3% to 18% of its overall RMS level. So the cut-off is not a hard truncation of loud audio. One possible cause, which I did not test, is that I trimmed edge silence from the training clips, so the models learned to end abruptly, with low predicted energy on some final phones (3 in T1, 8 in T2b). The robotic quality may come partly from the universal HiFi-GAN vocoder not being fine-tuned, though I did not test this either.

Code version. The repository's commit history was consolidated before publication, so the commit named in section 8 is the published code, not a commit recorded during the training runs.

Limitations known before training

  • English speakers: Svarah as loaded has no speaker column. Filename fields are not speaker IDs, and the 115 metadata profiles were not used as identities. All clips train as one group without speaker embeddings, so an averaged voice is expected. Telugu uses one label, which is not the same condition, and this limits any comparison.
  • Telugu speaker: median F0 under the male label drifts from about 160 to 209 Hz across rows, which F0 alone cannot explain as one speaker or several.
  • English lexicon: preparation keeps the first listed pronunciation (good as G IH0 D); 1,018 of 6,571 corpus words have several. Abbreviations can be aligned as words, for example CE as S.
  • Telugu alignment: ny stays at one frame in 87.7% of 1,458 cases, and 7 kept clips contain Latin letters. The English topology fix was not applied to Telugu. The model predicts one frame for ny in T1, T2b and T4, though I heard no problem in those words.
  • Failed alignments: 41 English and 47 Telugu clips have no alignment, for unknown reasons.
  • English frontend: AM becomes a m and is read as AH0 EH1 M, and hyphens in number words add pauses. Both are visible in E4.
  • Vocoder: universal HiFi-GAN is not fine-tuned to either dataset. On real mels, log-mel L1 was 0.43 to 0.53, which does not establish perceived quality.
  • Homographs: Telugu పట్టు has two meanings with the same text-derived phones, so T2a and T2b can differ only through context.

Possible improvements

  • Keep a short silence at clip ends during training, to test whether this fixes the cut-off last words.
  • Read abbreviations such as AM by letter names, and stop hyphens adding pauses in number words.
  • Keep the best validation checkpoint as well as the latest. It was lost here, and both models stopped improving long before the end. Regularization or early stopping should also be tested.
  • Keep multiple English pronunciations during alignment, and apply the topology change to Telugu.
  • Use verified speaker IDs with speaker embeddings for Svarah.
  • Fine-tune HiFi-GAN on each dataset.
  • Check the Telugu rules against a reference lexicon.

8. Reproducibility

Dependencies are in requirements.txt; MFA 3.4.3 has its own environment created by scripts/run_mfa.sh. Configurations are English/config.yaml and Telugu/config.yaml. Set HF_TOKEN for gated Svarah access, after accepting its terms.

pip install -r requirements.txt
python English/preprocess.py     # or Telugu/; --start and --stop choose steps
python English/train.py          # rerunning resumes from the newest checkpoint
python English/inference.py      # writes Results/English/
python scripts/collect_deliverables.py
python -m pytest -q tests

Colab notebooks in notebooks/ run the same steps. Selection is seeded with 1234. Upstream commit: d4e79eb. {{CODE_COMMIT}}
